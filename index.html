What Are You Looking At?

You are watching a real-time numerical solution to the Navier-Stokes equations, the fundamental laws of fluid dynamics. The colors you see represent "dye" or "ink" trapped in the fluid. The brighter and more vibrant the cyan/blue colors, the faster the fluid is moving in that area.

The Four Scenarios:

  • Lid-Driven Cavity: Imagine a square box full of water. The top wall is a treadmill moving constantly to the right. The friction from the top drags the fluid, creating a massive central swirl (vortex) and smaller secondary swirls in the bottom corners.
  • Central Ink Jet: Fluid is being blasted continuously from the bottom center into a stationary pool. Notice how the straight stream eventually wobbles and breaks apart into spinning curls.
  • Colliding Vortices: Two spinning pools of fluid are placed side by side. Notice how they interact, deform each other, and merge. In 2D fluids, smaller swirls tend to combine into bigger ones.
  • Turbulent Noise: The simulation starts with completely random, chaotic movements everywhere. Over time, friction (viscosity) smooths out the tiny jitters, and larger, organized spinning structures emerge from the chaos.

The $1 Million Millennium Prize Problem

The Navier-Stokes equations were written in the 1800s. We use them to design airplanes, predict the weather, and model ocean currents. They work incredibly well. But mathematically, we still don't fully understand them. The Clay Mathematics Institute has offered a $1,000,000 prize to anyone who can prove a specific property about them in 3D.

The Difference Between 2D and 3D

In 2D (Flat space), fluid is trapped on a plane. Swirls can only spin like coins on a table. The math here is "well-behaved." The energy stays manageable, and the equations always give a smooth, realistic answer.

In 3D (Real life), fluid dynamics gets nasty due to a phenomenon called Vortex Stretching. Imagine a swirling tornado of water. If the currents around it pull the top and bottom of that tornado apart, it stretches out. Just like an ice skater pulling their arms in, a stretched vortex spins faster. It gets thinner and faster, stretching endlessly.

The Problem: The equations suggest that this vortex stretching could theoretically cascade into an infinitely thin, infinitely fast spinning point. If velocity reaches infinity, the math "blows up" (breaks down). The million-dollar question is: Does the natural friction (viscosity) of the fluid always stop this from happening, or do the equations actually allow for impossible, infinite velocities?
